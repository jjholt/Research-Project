\section{Prostheses}
\subsection{Stump prosthesis}
Following amputation, prostheses are used to restore function.
Traditionally, these are stump-socket devices, where the residual limb (stump) is secured within the socket to provide attachment of the prosthesis.
For lower limb amputees, the reduced loads experienced by the skeleton lead to loss of bone density, and friction at the stump-socket interface frequently results in severe and chronic soft abrasion \citep{levy_skin_1980}.
This often prevents patients from being able to use their prosthesis and limits its long-term function.

\subsection{ITAP}
Intraosseous transcutaneous amputation prostheses (ITAP) \cite{kang_osseocutaneous_2010} are bone-anchored, skin-penetrating devices which provide an alternative means of attaching artificial limbs.
They overcome the problems associated with stump-socket devices by bypassing the role of the residual limb's soft tissue (See figure \ref{fig:ITAP}), whilst continuing to load the remaining bone, thus preventing reduction in bone mineral density.
The purpose of ITAP is to transfer the loads from the skeleton directly to the artificial limb, while simultaneously achieving cutaneous integration, i.e. avoiding infection and problems associated with breaching the skin barrier.

\section{Vibrotactile (haptic) feedback}
Amputation does not result in limb loss alone. It also results in the loss of tactile feedback; a fundamental way we gather information about the world around us.
Generally, prostheses users rely on visual feedback and secondary cues, such as sound or changes in pressure on the residual limb to recover the lost sensory information \cite{schofield_applications_2014}, suggesting an indirect level of proprioception at best.
Sensory feedback, particularly through the use of vibrations (i.e. vibrotactile), is associated with better object recognition, manipulation and grip force control \cite{schiefer_artificial_2018, rosenbaum-chou_development_2016}.
For stump-socket prostheses, sensory feedback must be achieved through electrical stimulation of soft tissue.
We hypothesise that bones' ability to perceive vibrations via osseoperception \cite{OrgelMarcus2021Oito}, combined with ITAP, could provide haptic feedback, further improving function following amputation.

\subsection{Clinically relevant frequencies}
In order to overcome limitations of vibration motors and a patient's ability to resolve discrete frequencies, there is motivation to use both frequency and particular vibration patterns, e.g. ascending amplitudes, pauses between vibrations, etc.   
People's ability to distinguish vibration patterns has been recognised \cite{ChaubeyPravin2014Cvhf}, and can bypass some of the traditional challenges of mounting vibration motors on skin by placing them on the ITAP. 
For soft tissue low-frequency stimulations, patients can distinguish between 1 Hz and 2 Hz with an accuracy of 71\% \cite{ChaubeyPravin2014Cvhf}, while in the range of \SIrange{60}{360}{Hz} patients have been shown to be able to distinguish 6 levels of vibration \cite{pylatiuk_progress_2004}.
It is unclear whether osseoperception allows for similar resolution, however studies suggest the use of frequencies in the range of \SIrange{1}{250}{Hz} \cite{jacobs_evaluation_2000}.

\section{Project goals}
ITAP can be surgically press-fit or cemented into the remaining bone depending on the quality of the bone residuum.
It was unclear what effect this has on vibrations propagating through ITAP or how they were transmitted to the bone and surrounding soft tissue.
The overarching aim of this thesis was to \textbf{characterise vibrations propagating through ITAP} as an initial step in developing haptic feedback.
The research questions addressed were: \begin{enumerate}
    \item How do vibrations propagate through ITAP?
    \item How can we control the amplitude of vibrations?
\end{enumerate}

These results fed into a larger research project that initially aimed to ensure the safety, efficacy and reliability of haptic feedback, and ultimately to develop more sophisticated cognitive control over artificial limbs.

\section{Methods}
This project investigated the propagation of vibrations along an ITAP by generating a computer aided design (CAD) model (Abaqus, Dassault Systemes).
It used a modal analysis to help inform the appropriate frequency range, then applied a concentrated point-force on the spigot, that prescribed a linear displacement according to equation \ref{eq:sin-eq}, where $\textbf{s}$ was displacement, $ A $ was amplitude, $t$ was time, $f$ was linear frequency, $z$ is a Cartesian axis.
Both modal analysis and prescribed linear displacement were carried out using Abaqus/Standard.

\begin{equation} \label{eq:sin-eq}
    \textbf{s}_z(t) = A\cos(2\pi f t)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Variables}
We explored the effects of each of the following independent variables as they pertained to the periodic force input: frequency, amplitude, duration and position.

\subsection{CAD Model}
A model ITAP was designed with intermedullary stem length of \SI{120}{mm}, proximal stem radius \SI{6}{mm}, tapering to \SI{4.5}{mm} distally.
A \SI{6}{mm} high, \SI{14}{mm} radius collar sat on the distal bone cortex.
An external spigot had a height of \SI{45}{mm} and radius of \SI{9}{mm}.

The CAD model had a hexahedral-dominated mesh (element types C3D8R and C3D6) and properties that of Titanium 6--4, i.e., elastic material of density \SI{4430}{\kilo\gram\per\metre\cubed}, Young's modulus \SI{114}{\giga\pascal} and Poisson's ratio 0.33.
