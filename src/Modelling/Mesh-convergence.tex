To find an appropriate balance between computational (CPU) time and accuracy of the solution, we performed a mesh convergence.
We compared the same simulation using an increasingly finer mesh, which is presented in figure \ref{fig:mesh-convergence}.

The results are tabulated in table \ref{tb:mesh-convergence}.
The total displacements for the coarsest and finest mesh were within 10\% of each other, but the increase in computational time was considerable.
These are relative numbers, and were we to have used the finest mesh size (\SI{1.25}{mm}) in our modelling, extensive computational time would have compromised the amount of data we could obtain.
Between the mesh sizes of \SI{2.5}{mm} and \SI{1.25}{mm}, the difference in displacement was negligible, despite the significant increase in computational time.
We decided to use the \SI{2.5}{mm} mesh, which resulted in a model with 4300 elements.

\begin{table} \centering
    \begin{tabular}{ccc}
        \textbf{Mesh size [\si{\milli\metre}]} & \textbf{Total CPU time [\si{\second}]} & \textbf{Displacement, magnitude [\si{\nano\metre}]}\\ \hline
          10 & 14  & 68\\
           5 & 17  & 67\\
         2.5 & 47  & 63\\
        1.25 & 303 & 62
    \end{tabular}
    \caption[Mesh convergence]{A comparison between computational time and accuracy that informed how a mesh size was chosen.}
    \label{tb:mesh-convergence}
\end{table}
