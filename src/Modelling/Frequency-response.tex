Based on the modal analysis we run simulations for frequencies from \SIrange{1}{900}{Hz} and track the maximum displacement, as can be seen in figure \ref{fig:max-displacement-6}.
At low frequencies, up to about \SI{400}{Hz}, we observe a linear decline of about 2 decade/decade, or -\SI{60}{\deci\bel\per decade} across the entire device.
As frequency increases to \SI{850}{Hz} we see the non-linear behaviour, wherein the relative difference between each sensor increases.
The relative movement between each part may be a tool for assuring the displacements are safe, therefore we explore it in further detail.

The spacing between frequencies is fairly uneven at low values, and perhaps could have been for example 10 or 20 divisions per decade.
This is only possible because the creation of the simulations and the data processing were completely automated with a Python script.
Ultimately it is not an issue because of the linear behaviour observed, but it should have been a design consideration.

\paragraph{Relative displacements}
Figure \ref{fig:normalisation} characterises the maximum displacement for all parts relative to the maximum displacement of the spigot.
For the clinically relevant frequencies, the displacement of the stem hovers nicely around 4 times that of the spigot.

At this point it is hard to say whether the large relative displacement observed around \SI{700}{Hz} is useful.
We may well want to take advantage of the large relative displacement if it translates to larger forces in our subsequent studies, though it likely comes with a loss in resolution.
For this reason we continue to characterise vibrations beyond what is clinically relevant.
